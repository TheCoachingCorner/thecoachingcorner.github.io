Find Your Position

Serves · Lesson 01 — Position Fundamentals 15 minutes 8+ players

Needs · 6 cones · a ball each · half a field · the six outfield position printouts, one per cone

intropositionscommunication

The big idea

KNOW YOUR SPOT → SAY IT OUT LOUD

Every rule in this drill exists to make somebody name their position while they are out of breath.

Set Up

Six cones in the shape they play in, a printout at each one, a ball each. No goalie cone — whoever keeps goal on Saturday is an outfield player here.

More players than cones is fine: share the cone, share the name. Leave the six outfield cards from lesson 01 at their cones, weighed down — wet grass eats paper, so bag them or treat them as consumable.

How It Runs

1 · The test

4 min
  1. Cards face down. Everyone on their cone.
  2. Ask each player: “What position are you?”
  3. Then: “Point at where your neighborhood ends.”
  4. Wrong answer, nothing happens — tell them, they say it back, move on.

Cards face up after this, and stay up.

2 · Sharks

8 min
  1. Everyone at a position with a ball. Two left over are sharks, no balls.
  2. A shark who dribbles your ball three times takes it — and your position. Kicking it away is nothing.
  3. You yell the position, they yell it back. Now you are the shark.
  4. Shout “POSITIONS!” — reach your cone and name it to be safe.
  5. Sharks hunt until everyone is safe.

What You Shout

“POSITIONS!” “Say it!” “Say it back!” “One, two, three!” “I can’t hear you!”

“Say it back!” is the half that gets skipped, every time, by the player who most needs to say it. “One, two, three!” is you counting a shark’s dribbles out loud, so the whole field learns what claiming a ball looks like.

Why it works Everything above is what you need on the grass. Below is the thinking behind it — read it once before the session, not during.

Why It Works

The repeat is the teaching “Right mid!” — “Right mid!”

The one who needs to remember the position is the shark, and saying a thing is what makes it stick — hearing it is not. It is also a free check: a player who cannot say it back did not have it, and you find out in the second it happens rather than next Saturday.

Where the reps come from “POSITIONS!”

A swap names one position, maybe five times a session. Every call names all of them at once, out loud, under pressure — and the ones who hesitate are audible. Safety at the cone is what makes it a race instead of an instruction.

Three dribbles “Catch it and keep it. Kicking it away is nothing.”

A rule kids adjudicate themselves ends an argument that “he got it off me” runs all session. It is also the defender card’s rule enforced by the game: control the ball, because kicking it away is a pass to the other team.

Where it came from Find Your Home, practice 1.

The same idea without the sharks, and without anyone having to say their position out loud.

Second Time Round

A different week

Once they can find their cone under pressure, the drill has more to give. Same set-up, one rule added.

The added rule

STAY IN YOUR NEIGHBORHOOD

On your honor. No penalty, nobody polices it.

Now the cone is not a place you run to, it is the middle of the area you live in — which is what lesson 01 is actually about.

Marking the zones “Your neighborhood is wherever your cone is the nearest cone.”

That draws all six for you, from the cones already on the grass. And it is a question a nine-year-old can answer while running — is my cone the closest one to me? — where am I in my zone? needs a referee.

The audit “POSITIONS!”

You do not have to watch the zones. When they all run home you can see who was nowhere near theirs — and that is the moment to say so, once, lightly.

It gets harder “You cannot run away any more.”

Holding an area, you have to shield and turn instead. That is the point, and it is why this waits for a later week.

Why honor and not a rule

A penalty would spiral. Leaving your zone is the mistake a keen player makes — the one who chases everything — and turning them into a shark for hustling is a hard thing to explain mid-drill. It also gives the game two ways in and one way out, and once the sharks outnumber the players nobody can get back.

Asking instead costs nothing and teaches the thing you actually want: a player who holds their shape when no one is looking.
