Find Your Position

Serves · Lesson 01 — Position Fundamentals 15 minutes 8+ players

Needs · 6 cones · a ball each · half a field · the six outfield position printouts, one per cone

intropositionscommunication

The big idea

KNOW YOUR SPOT → SAY IT OUT LOUD

Every rule in this drill exists to make somebody name their position while they are out of breath.

Set Up

A cone on each of the six field positions, in the shape they play in.

No keeper“Six positions, six cones.”

Whoever keeps goal on Saturday is an outfield player here. The goalie’s job is the one that isn’t a place, and that is lesson 02’s to teach.

More players than cones“Share the cone, share the name.”

Leave a printout at every cone

The card already carries the thing this drill is about — the neighborhood drawn on a field. A kid who has forgotten where left mid lives can look, instead of asking you across forty yards. Weigh each one down with its cone.

Print the six outfield cards from lesson 01 — not the goalie’s. Wet grass eats paper: sheet protector, freezer bag, or accept they are consumable. Decide before Saturday, not at the cone.

1 · The Test

Turn the cards face down first. They answer both questions, and a test you can read off the grass tests nothing.

Everyone on their cone

4 minutes

Go round and ask each player two things:

  1. “What position are you?”
  2. “Point at where your neighborhood ends.”

Naming it is the easy half. The pointing is where you find out whether they know it, and it is worth the extra thirty seconds a player.

Get it wrong and nothing happens — you tell them, they say it back, move on. This is a check on your teaching, not a test they can fail.

Then turn the cards face up and leave them there for the rest of the drill.

2 · Sharks

Pick two to start as sharks. Everyone else has a ball and dribbles anywhere in the area; sharks have no ball and try to win one.

Losing your ball

Say it both ways

The player who lost it yells the position. The shark who took it yells it back.

“Right mid!” — “Right mid!”

That shark is now the right mid, with a ball. The player who lost it is now a shark. Neither of them moves until both have said it.

The repeat is the half that teaches. The one who needs to remember the position is the shark, and saying a thing is what makes it stick — hearing it is not. It is also a free check: a player who cannot say it back did not have it, and you find that out in the second it happens rather than next Saturday.

The call

Any time

“POSITIONS!”

Everyone with a ball runs for their cone — and the sharks keep hunting. The call is not a freeze. It is a race home while two people are chasing you.

Getting to your cone is not enough. You have to name it. Reach it with your ball, yell your position, and you are safe. Arrive silently and you are still fair game. Near the cone is close enough — this is not a race to stand on a disc.

Sharks have no cone, which is the point: there is nowhere for them to be safe, and the only way out is to win a ball and inherit somebody’s position.

Sharks keep hunting until everyone is safe. That is what ends the round — not a whistle, and not you counting down.

Hold them there a moment — long enough to look around and see the shape they are standing in — then send them back out.

This is where the reps come from. A swap names one position, maybe five times a session. Every “POSITIONS!” names all of them at once, out loud, under pressure — and the ones who hesitate are audible.

Winning The Ball

The rule

DRIBBLE IT THREE TIMES

Three touches, under control, moving with it. Then it is yours.

Kicking it away is nothing. A shark who pokes your ball into the next field has won nothing — they have to go and get it, and you are still a player with a position.

This is the same thing the defender card says — control the ball; kicking it away is a pass to the other team. Here the game enforces it instead of a coach repeating it: a shark who clears it stays a shark.

This is the only way to become a shark. Not leaving your area, not being last home, not losing your ball out of bounds.

What You Shout

“POSITIONS!” “Say it!” “Say it back!” “One, two, three!” “I can’t hear you!”

“Say it back!” is the half that gets skipped, every time, by the player who most needs to say it. “One, two, three!” is you counting a shark’s dribbles out loud, so the whole field learns what claiming a ball looks like.

Second Time Round

A different week

Once they can find their cone under pressure, the drill has more to give. Same set-up, one rule added.

The added rule

STAY IN YOUR NEIGHBORHOOD

On your honor. No penalty, nobody polices it.

Now the cone is not a place you run to, it is the middle of the area you live in — which is what lesson 01 is actually about.

Marking the zones “Your neighborhood is wherever your cone is the nearest cone.”

That draws all six for you, from the cones already on the grass. And it is a question a nine-year-old can answer while running — is my cone the closest one to me? — where am I in my zone? needs a referee.

The audit “POSITIONS!”

You do not have to watch the zones. When they all run home you can see who was nowhere near theirs — and that is the moment to say so, once, lightly.

It gets harder “You cannot run away any more.”

Holding an area, you have to shield and turn instead. That is the point, and it is why this waits for a later week.

Why honor and not a rule

A penalty would spiral. Leaving your zone is the mistake a keen player makes — the one who chases everything — and turning them into a shark for hustling is a hard thing to explain mid-drill. It also gives the game two ways in and one way out, and once the sharks outnumber the players nobody can get back.

Asking instead costs nothing and teaches the thing you actually want: a player who holds their shape when no one is looking.
