Cross-Fire

Serves · Lesson 10 — Passing Fundamentals (no page yet) 8 minutes 8–12, all in at once

Needs · three more cones than players, one or two balls, a circle 12 to 15 yards across

intropassing

The big idea

LOOK → PASS THROUGH → MOVE

Never to your neighbour, so the ball has to cross the middle — and so does everybody else.

1

Set Up

A ring of cones 12 to 15 yards across, one player on a cone. Leave three cones empty — that is what there is to move to, and without them nobody can go anywhere.

2

The Only Rule

Pass across. Never to your neighbour.

Your neighbour is the cone either side of you. Everything else is fair game, and the ball has to cross the middle to get there. After you pass, go to an open cone.

3

How It Runs

  1. One ball. Pass across, not to your neighbour. Nobody moves yet.
  2. Add the move. Pass, then go find an open cone.
  3. Two balls. Now they have to look before they pass and before they run.
  4. The challenge — “how many passes can we make in thirty seconds?” Count out loud, everybody. Play it twice and try to beat the first number.

Ball out of the circle? Whoever is nearest grabs it and carries on. Do not restart, do not stop to explain. A warm-up that keeps stopping is not warming anybody up.

4

When Somebody Gets Hit

Balls cross the middle and so do people. It will happen.

Ball hits a runner? Five toe taps — both of you.

Foot on top of the ball, tap tap tap, standing still. Five seconds and back in. Both of you, every time — the passer did not look and the runner did not look, so there is nothing to argue about and nobody to blame. It also stops it being a punishment for passing hard, which would only teach them not to pass.

5

Monkey In The Middle

Two rounds in, one player steps into the circle. Nothing else changes — same cones, same rule, same movement.

Touch the ball and you are out. The monkey takes the passer’s cone, and whoever played that pass goes in the middle.

1One monkey
swap on every touch
2Two monkeys
once one is too easy

No timer and no score — the swap is the whole game. The circle is now doing the real job: they are looking through pressure instead of passing around it.

Coaches: this is a rondo. Never say that word to them — they already know this game.

6

Modifiers

Easier
  • One ball the whole time.
  • Bigger circle — longer passes, thinner traffic.
  • Walk the movement instead of running it.
Harder
  • Three balls.
  • Two touches only — one to control, one to pass.
  • Call their name before you pass it.
  • Weak foot only.
  • Shrink the circle.
Instead of toe taps
  • The team's count goes back five. Nobody pays anything on their own, and the count is already the thing they care about.
7

What You Shout

“Head up!”“Across, not next door!” “Find a space!”“Count it!”
8

What Did We Learn

At the water break, not on the grass.

  • What made a pass easy to receive?
  • When there were two balls, what did you have to do differently?
  • With a monkey in the middle — where did the open player keep turning up?
  • What happened when you passed without looking first?
  • Saturday: you get the ball with your back to their goal. What is the first thing you do?
Why it works Everything above is what you need on the grass. Below is the thinking behind it — read it once before the session, not during.

Why It Works

More cones than players is the whole design

With one cone each there is nowhere to go, so the movement half of the drill quietly disappears and it becomes a passing circle. Three spare cones means there is always somewhere to be, and choosing which open cone is a decision they make every few seconds without being asked to.

Not-your-neighbour forces the head up

A pass to the person beside you can be made without looking, which is exactly the habit we are trying not to build. Making the ball cross the middle means the passer has to find somebody first.

The second ball is what makes it worth doing

One ball is a passing drill where nine children stand still. Two means everybody is watching all the time, because the one you cannot see is the one that hits you.

The count belongs to the team

A child who mis-passes has not lost anything of their own — and everybody hears the number, so nobody has to referee it.

The forfeit is deliberately tiny and shared

Five toe taps is five seconds, done standing up so nobody is on the ground getting trodden on, and it is a touch on the ball rather than a punishment exercise. Making both players do it removes the argument about whose fault it was, which is the only way a rule like this survives contact with ten nine-year-olds.

Swapping on a touch runs itself

No timer to watch and nothing to keep score of — the monkey gets out by earning it, and the player who lost the ball goes in. It also puts the consequence on the pass rather than on the defending, which is the half of it we are actually trying to improve.

The name does the explaining

Monkey in the middle is a game they have played since they were five, so the rules arrive for free and nobody has to be told what a rondo is. Same circle, same rule, same movement — one child steps in and every pass has to be found through somebody.

Open Questions

  • Three spare cones is a guess. Two might be enough with one ball; four might be needed with three. Watch whether anybody is standing still with nowhere to go.
  • Twelve yards across for 10U. Long enough to need a proper pass, short enough that a weak one still arrives. Untested.
  • Whether the toe-tap forfeit is needed at all. It might be funnier than it is useful, and the team-count version may do the same job with less stopping.
  • Whether the monkeys belong in the warm-up slot. Once there is somebody in the middle it is a rondo, and it may want its own place in the session rather than the first eight minutes.
